A personal and scientific journey

My Journey

My path has rarely been straight. That became one of its strengths.

Movement, unfamiliar environments, curiosity, and repeated beginnings have shaped the way I approach both life and science. Long before I entered a laboratory, I was learning how to adapt, observe, rebuild familiarity, and continue moving forward.

Twelve Schools, One Continuing Journey

Because of my parents' frequent relocations and travel, I attended 12 different schools during my childhood and adolescence.

Each transition meant unfamiliar classrooms, different expectations, new friendships, and the need to understand another environment. For a long time, every move felt like beginning again.

Eventually I understood that I was never actually starting from zero.

I carried something from every previous place with me. I learned how to observe before judging, communicate with different people, adapt without losing my identity, and create a sense of belonging through contribution rather than familiarity.

My parents were my first example of what consistency really means. Watching them manage change, responsibility, travel, and uncertainty taught me that dedication is not about having ideal circumstances. It is about continuing to fulfill your responsibilities when the circumstances are not ideal.

Those repeated beginnings became preparation for almost everything that came later.

Moving between countries, entering unfamiliar academic systems, transitioning into research, working across disciplines, and building a life in Japan all required the same ability I had unknowingly been practicing since childhood: adapt, learn, contribute, and continue.

Dentistry Was the Beginning, Not the Boundary

Dentistry gave me my first serious understanding of human biology, disease, surgery, anatomy, healing, and the responsibility involved in treating another person.

But the more I learned clinically, the more interested I became in questions that treatment alone could not completely answer.

Why does one tissue regenerate while another heals incompletely?

Why can bone restore itself in one situation but fail in another?

Could we understand biological systems well enough to guide regeneration rather than simply repair the damage afterward?

Dentistry gradually led me from treating structures to questioning how those structures are built, maintained, damaged, and rebuilt.

That curiosity eventually pulled me toward tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

From Repair to Regeneration

I joined the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at the Graduate School of Medicine, The University of Tokyo, and began studying bone not simply as a structure, but as a living and continuously changing system.

Bone is never truly still.

Cells form it, remove it, reorganize it, communicate through it, and continually respond to biological and physical signals. Understanding those interactions became the central theme of my doctoral work.

I worked with a three dimensional functional bone model that allowed bone formation, resorption, and subsequent reformation to be studied within the same experimental system.

Rather than examining one isolated endpoint, I became interested in how one biological event changes the events that follow it.

The question became not only what happened, but when, where, and what happened next.

This perspective shaped my work on parathyroid hormone exposure patterns and their influence on the coupling between bone resorption and reformation.

That phase of my doctoral research culminated in a study published in Scientific Reports in 2026.

More importantly, it changed the way I think about biological regulation. A signal cannot always be understood only by asking whether it is present or absent. Its timing, duration, frequency, location, and cellular context can completely change its effect.

Learning to Watch Biology Move

One of the most important changes in my scientific thinking came when I stopped being satisfied with static images.

I became fascinated not only by what a cell is, but by what it does.

Where does it move?

How does its morphology change?

Which neighbouring cells influence its behaviour?

How do thousands of local interactions become a tissue-level change?

This interest drew me toward longitudinal imaging, two photon microscopy, three dimensional analysis, and quantitative methods.

Imaging allowed me to observe biological events that would otherwise remain hidden. Computational analysis allowed me to turn those observations into measurable relationships.

Observation shows that something happened. Measurement helps reveal how it happened.

The combination of experimental biology, imaging, and quantitative analysis became one of the strongest influences on the kind of researcher I want to become.

Curiosity Beyond the Laboratory

I have always been conscious of what I do not yet know.

Whenever I recognized a missing skill, my instinct was usually to explore it rather than accept it as a permanent limitation.

That habit led me outside my formal training.

I began learning coding because I wanted greater control over data, analysis, visualization, and digital tools. Website development followed because I wanted to understand how complete digital experiences were built rather than depend entirely on templates.

I explored Photoshop, Canva, and visual design because communicating an idea clearly can be almost as important as developing the idea itself.

Architecture and nature influenced me in a different way.

Architecture showed me how individual elements can be organized into structures that are stable, functional, and adaptive.

Nature showed me something more complex: structures that build, repair, reorganize, and respond to their environment without a central designer controlling every step.

Bone sits somewhere inside that fascination.

Its function depends not only on what it is made from, but on how its components organize, communicate, remodel, and adapt over time.

Coding taught me to think in systems. Design taught me to think about clarity. Architecture taught me to think about structure. Biology taught me that structure can also be alive.

What Dedication Came to Mean

My definition of dedication has changed over time.

It is no longer something I associate with working intensely for a short period.

Research taught me a different form of persistence.

Experiments fail. Assumptions prove incomplete. Results contradict expectations. Manuscripts return with criticism. Opportunities are sometimes lost despite serious preparation.

Failure is not always the opposite of progress.

An unsuccessful experiment can expose a technical limitation. An unexpected result can reveal a better question. Criticism can expose weaknesses that would otherwise remain hidden.

My teachers, supervisors, colleagues, and scientific mentors taught me that good work requires patience, accuracy, humility, critical thinking, and the willingness to revise.

Scholarships, scientific meetings, international experiences, and opportunities to present my work also reminded me that progress is rarely individual.

Every meaningful stage has depended on people who taught me, challenged me, trusted me, supported me, or gave me an opportunity.

Dedication, for me, became the commitment to keep becoming more capable, more precise, and more useful through my work.

The Direction Ahead

I do not see my future as the continuation of one experiment or one technique.

I am interested in biological systems in which cells communicate, reorganize, regenerate, age, respond to perturbation, and collectively produce changes that cannot be understood by studying each component independently.

Tissue engineering, organoid models, advanced microscopy, molecular biology, quantitative imaging, computational analysis, and emerging spatial and single-cell technologies provide different ways of approaching those questions.

The methods may change. The underlying curiosity is more constant.

I want to understand living systems deeply enough to influence their behaviour in useful and predictable ways, particularly where that understanding can contribute to regeneration, disease modelling, therapeutic discovery, and eventually better clinical solutions.

I also want to continue crossing boundaries between fields rather than defining myself entirely by one of them.

Clinician. Researcher. Tissue engineer. Programmer. Designer. Learner.

I do not see these as separate identities. Each one changes how I approach the others.

The goal is not to know everything. It is to keep expanding what I am capable of understanding and building.

Advice to My Younger Self

Do not be afraid of beginning again.

You will enter new schools, cities, countries, laboratories, and unfamiliar environments. Sometimes it will appear that everyone else already knows where they belong while you are still trying to find your place.

Do not confuse unfamiliarity with failure.

You do not need to understand your entire future before taking the next step. Dentistry will lead you somewhere you did not originally expect. Research will create new questions. Some plans will change. Some opportunities will disappear. Others will arrive before you feel ready.

Take the step anyway.

Learn to value consistency more than motivation. Motivation comes and goes. Consistent effort will carry you through difficult experiments, rejection, uncertainty, and periods when progress feels invisible.

Do not fear mistakes. Examine them honestly and make them useful.

Listen carefully to people with more experience, but do not surrender your curiosity. Confidence without humility stops learning. Humility without confidence stops action. You will need both.

Remember the people who supported you. Very little that matters is achieved completely alone.

And do not wait until you feel completely prepared. Readiness often develops after the first step, not before it.

One day you will understand that the movement, uncertainty, mistakes, and repeated beginnings were not interruptions to the journey.

They were the preparation.

The journey continues
